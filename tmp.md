Model Development has defined methodological tests covering the use of overlapping windows (MT.1), the 10-year credit spread history (MT.2), time period selection (MT.3), time bucket granularity (MT.4), rating-bucket granularity (MT.5), benchmarking the CSRBB methodology (MT.6) and sensitivity analysis (MT.7). Section 6.1.2 of the TDD describes the purpose, design, success criteria, execution and results of these tests. The validator considers these tests relevant and assesses the documented results and their scope below.

MT.1 — Overlapping time windows
MT.1 assesses the impact of overlapping one-year credit spread windows in the CSRBB calculation. The test includes an autocorrelation assessment of the resulting scenario-loss series, a comparison using gaps of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months and 1 year, and comparisons of overlapping and non-overlapping observations for different shock horizons. The documented success criterion is that overlapping windows produce estimates at least as high as those obtained without overlap.

The autocorrelation analysis confirms dependence in the scenario-loss series. Table 41 reports a baseline estimate of EUR 22.44 million, compared with EUR 21.87 million for the one-week gap and approximately EUR 17.12 million for the one-year gap. The documented comparisons meet the stated success criterion. The validator considers the gap analysis relevant sensitivity evidence. However, the number of scenarios falls from 2,595 in the baseline to 10 for the one-year gap, so the comparison combines changes in overlap with changes in the sampled observations. It does not isolate the effect of autocorrelation or establish that overlapping windows are conservative for every portfolio.

The MT.1 narrative on TDD page 92 describes the one-week result as a 2.55% increase, while Table 41 reports a 2.55% decrease. This reporting inconsistency is raised under documentation Issue #1.

MT.2 — Presence of stressed periods in the historical sample
MT.2 assesses whether the selected 10-year credit spread history includes periods of significant volatility. The test combines a graphical review of the scenario-loss series with a comparison of annual volatility. Each annual volatility is divided by that of the calmest year, with a ratio above three used to identify significant volatility. The TDD acknowledges that portfolio offsets can conceal movements in the underlying curves and provides for additional curve-level analysis where the portfolio diagnostic shows no significant volatility.

The graphical review identifies periods of market stress, and Table 43 reports several annual volatility ratios above three, with a maximum of 5.20. The TDD also refers to analysis of annual credit spread changes in Appendix A.7. The validator considers the documented results to support the presence of non-calm periods in the development sample. The volatility ratio is a relative diagnostic against the calmest year, and its interpretation remains dependent on the tested portfolio and historical period.

MT.3 — Historical observation period
MT.3 assesses the sensitivity of the model to the selected historical period. The model is run using credit spread histories of 8, 10, 12 and 15 years, all ending on 31 December 2025, with other inputs unchanged. The revised TDD defines a 10% underestimation threshold for comparison with the alternative periods. Table 44 reports estimates between EUR 22.25 million and EUR 22.54 million, compared with the 10-year baseline of EUR 22.44 million. All differences are below 1% and within the documented tolerance.

The validator considers the test relevant and the results supportive of low sensitivity to the tested lookback lengths for the Q4 2025 portfolio. The windows share the same end date and much of the same history. The results therefore demonstrate stability across these selected histories, rather than stability when a dominant stress episode leaves a rolling window.

MT.4 — Bucket midpoints and time-bucket granularity
MT.4 assesses the use of bucket midpoints for discounting and reviews the maturity distribution within the existing time buckets. The test uses cashflow histograms and compares midpoint time factors with cashflow-weighted average maturities. Weighted averages are calculated for assets and liabilities separately and on a combined basis. The capital comparison uses the combined weighted-average maturity for each bucket. The revised TDD defines success as an increase in CSRBB of no more than 10% when weighted-average maturities replace midpoints.

Table 46 reports an increase from EUR 22,444,908.88 to EUR 23,406,040.93, or 4.28%, which is below the documented threshold. The validator considers this a relevant sensitivity test that supports the midpoint approximation for the tested portfolio. The histogram review provides additional information on cashflow concentrations. The test does not directly measure the capital effect of finer bucket segmentation or changes to the mapped spread and interest-rate tenors, so its numerical conclusion is limited to the midpoint-versus-weighted-average comparison.

MT.5 — Rating mapping
MT.5 assesses the effect of mapping an internal credit grade to an external rating where more than one external rating is available. The test compares the closest-PD baseline with lower-rating and upper-rating alternatives. The revised criterion compares the baseline with each alternative and requires the baseline estimate to be no more than 10% below the alternative estimate. Table 48 reports EUR 22,444,908.88 for all three mappings.

The TDD explains that the Q4 2025 portfolio contains no cashflows assigned to internal grades for which the mapping alternatives differ. The validator considers the comparison relevant, but the identical results demonstrate no impact for the tested portfolio rather than the relative performance of the mapping choices for affected grades. The test therefore provides limited evidence for portfolios containing those grades.

MT.6 — Benchmark against the previous CSRBB methodology
MT.6 compares the Q4 2025 estimate under the new model with the previous CSRBB methodology. The TDD defines a deviation of more than 10% in either direction as a trigger for detailed analysis. Table 50 reports EUR 18,376,370.80 under the previous methodology and EUR 22,444,908.88 under the new model, an increase of EUR 4,068,538.08, or 22.14%. The trigger is therefore exceeded, and the TDD analyses differences in cashflows and instrument scope, time buckets, rating granularity, shock methodology and historical period.

The validator considers this benchmark relevant for explaining the change in the reported estimate. Table 51 varies each methodological feature separately against the new-model baseline; these effects are not additive and do not provide a numerical reconciliation of the total 22.14% increase. In addition, the shock comparison replaces the former separate asset and liability shocks with a common cashflow-weighted shock. It therefore provides an approximate comparison with the former shock approach. The results provide useful evidence of the model’s response to the main methodological changes, subject to these limits on attribution.

The MT.6 narrative on TDD page 107 states an increase of 22.74%, while Table 50 and the reported amounts give 22.14%. This reporting inconsistency is raised under documentation Issue #1.

MT.7 — Sensitivity analysis
MT.7 assesses the direction, magnitude and shape of the model’s response to changes in asset cashflows, liability cashflows, historical credit spread shocks, reporting-date credit spread levels and interest rates. Each active input is varied separately, with result tables covering ±5%, ±10%, ±15% and ±20%. The interest-rate adjustment is a parallel shift equal to the curve mean multiplied by the selected scale. Beta factors and beta-sensitive cashflows are excluded because they are inactive in the current model. The success criterion is that the observed responses are explainable and do not indicate unexpected model behaviour; no fixed numerical tolerance is applied.

For the tested portfolio, increasing asset cashflows reduces the estimate, while increasing liability cashflows raises it. The TDD explains these responses through the liability surplus and cashflow offsets. Increasing reporting-date credit spread levels raises the estimate, while the tested upward interest-rate shifts reduce it. The response to scaling historical spread shocks is non-monotonic. The TDD attributes this behaviour to the zero floor on shocked credit spreads and provides a rerun without the floor, which shows a positive relationship between shock scale and the estimate.

The validator considers the analysis relevant for understanding the model’s response and recognises the additional evidence from the no-floor rerun. An explainability criterion is appropriate for this test. The reported directions relate to the tested portfolio; the convexity of an individual discount factor does not by itself establish the behaviour of the net portfolio quantile, which also reflects signed cashflows and the ranking of scenarios.

The interest-rate summary in Table 58 does not agree with the detailed results in Table 52. For example, the +10% case is shown as −0.71% in Table 58, while Table 52 reports EUR 22,127,655.04, equivalent to −1.41% against the baseline. The descriptions of the spread-level and interest-rate results also use “not monotonic” for the displayed nonlinear but monotonic responses. These reporting inconsistencies are raised under documentation Issue #1.
